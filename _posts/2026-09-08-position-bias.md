_Studying LLM Behavior · Behavioral demonstration_

Language models are increasingly being used to evaluate the responses of other
language models. The judge receives a question and two answers, then decides
which answer is better. This makes large evaluations easier to run, but it also

answers appeared in the opposite order?

Shi et al. studied this question across several judge models and tasks. They
found that reversing the answers could change which one was preferred, and
that the size and direction of this position effect varied across models and
evaluation settings. The effect was generally larger when the two answers were
closer in quality ([Shi et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07791)).

The demonstration adapts the answer-swapping procedure to a small subset of
MT-Bench. It retains the published questions, model answers, and central swap,
while using fewer items and a different judge model.

> **This is a pipeline demonstration, not a standalone benchmark study.** Its
> purpose is to show how a published procedure can be constructed, run, and
> recorded with the infrastructure developed in this series. The small sample
> and single judge model are not intended as a replication test, an extension
> study, or an estimate of the prevalence or size of position effects across
> LLMs.

## The answers change position, not identity

For every question, the judge sees the same pair of answers twice. Only their
positions change.

{% include figure.liquid

  caption="Figure 1. The same two answers are presented in their original and reversed positions."

Suppose the underlying answers are X and Y. If the judge selects A when X is in
position A, then selects B after X moves to position B, the visible letter has
changed but the preferred content has not. The choice is content-consistent
across positions. If the judge selects A in both orders, it has switched from X
to Y and has favored the first position.

The analysis therefore cannot treat A and B as the outcomes. Each letter has
to be mapped back to the answer occupying that position.

The swap makes it possible to distinguish three different patterns:

- **Repetition stability:** Does the judge choose the same A/B positional
  option when an identical trial is repeated?
- **Answer-identity consistency across positions:** Does the judge continue to
  prefer the same underlying answer, X or Y, when that answer moves to a
  different A/B position? Shi et al. call this measure _position consistency_.
- **Positional preference:** When its choice changes, does it tend to favor the
  first answer or the second?

A judge can be highly stable and still be sensitive to position. It could, for
example, consistently choose whichever answer appears first.

## What was retained from the published procedure

Zheng et al. introduced MT-Bench to evaluate chatbot responses, including
comparisons made by an LLM judge
([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). The public FastChat
repository contains the questions, judge prompts, evaluation code, and
instructions for retrieving pre-generated model answers
([MT-Bench repository](https://github.com/lm-sys/FastChat/tree/main/fastchat/llm_judge)).

The demonstration uses 12 MT-Bench questions: three each from writing,
reasoning, STEM, and humanities. The original MT-Bench archive contains
responses generated by several models. For each question, the response recorded
for Vicuna is paired separately with the responses recorded for Alpaca,
GPT-3.5, and GPT-4, producing 36 pairs. The questions and comparison models were
selected before the Qwen responses were examined.

Each pair appears in both orders, with each order repeated three times. The
published MT-Bench judge prompt already includes an instruction telling the
judge not to allow answer order to influence its decision. That instruction is
important here because the experiment is testing whether order nevertheless
changes which underlying answer content is preferred.

The published prompt is compared with a second version in which only this
warning has been removed.

This type of comparison is called an **ablation**. One component is removed
while the rest of the procedure remains unchanged. Here, the removed component
is a single instruction telling the judge not to let answer order influence its
decision. Comparing the two prompts asks whether that warning changes which
underlying answer the judge selects.

| Prompt condition | What the judge receives               | Role in this study         |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Original         | The position-bias warning is retained | Published prompt condition |
| Warning ablation | Only the warning sentence is removed  | Demonstration manipulation |

This is a demonstration manipulation rather than a novel extension. Zheng et
al. had already tested a shorter prompt that removed the warning along with
several other instructions.

The complete schedule contains:

```text
12 questions × 3 answer pairs × 2 orders
× 2 prompt conditions × 3 repetitions = 432 judgments
```

Each judgment is sent as a new request, and the trial order is randomized. The
two versions of a pair can therefore appear anywhere in the sequence rather
than being administered as a fixed pair of consecutive trials.

## The materials were checked before data collection

The 12 questions were chosen before Qwen was run as the judge. The audit then
confirmed that each question had the required archived responses from Vicuna,
Alpaca, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4, and that the prompt for comparing those responses
was complete. Only after these checks were finished were the pairs sent to
Qwen. This order prevents the questions from being chosen because they produced
a preferred result.

Each MT-Bench question in this demonstration contains an initial question and
a follow-up. The audit confirmed that every selected model answer responded to
both parts. It also confirmed that writing, reasoning, STEM, and humanities
were equally represented.

At the end of each judgment, Qwen reports its choice using a verdict marker.
`[[A]]` means that the first answer was selected, while `[[B]]` means that the
second answer was selected. The original MT-Bench format also defines `[[C]]`
as a tie, although the forced-choice prompt used here asks Qwen to choose only A
or B.

The parser looks for these markers in Qwen's response. Because a judge may quote
part of a candidate answer while explaining its decision, the audit checked
that none of the candidate answers already contained the same strings.
Otherwise, quoted text could be mistaken for the final verdict. All of the
prompts also fit within the configured context window.

## Build the schedule before sending any requests

The demonstration code and material instructions are in
[`llm-behavior-demos`](../../llm-behavior-demos/). Retrieve the pinned FastChat
files and four answer files, then place them in one input folder. Building the
schedule does not call a model.

> **Why are the answers not included in the repository?** The original model
> answers are hosted separately from the Apache-licensed FastChat code, and
> their page does not provide clear permission to redistribute them. The setup
> therefore downloads them from their original host. Their SHA-256 digests—a
> digital fingerprint for each file—are checked to make sure the intended files
> were retrieved. The details are preserved in the
> [`material audit`](../../llm-behavior-demos/materials/position-bias/AUDIT.md).

From the [`llm-behavior-demos`](../../llm-behavior-demos/) folder, run:

```bash
python -m position_bias_demo.build_schedule \
  --input-dir INPUT \
  --output-dir OUTPUT \
  --seed 20260815
```

Replace `INPUT` with the source-material folder and `OUTPUT` with the location
for the new schedule. The builder verifies the SHA-256 digest of every source
file and stops if a file has changed, a selected record is missing, or the
prompt cannot be modified as specified.

The output contains the trial schedule and a manifest recording the source
checksums, randomization seed, trial count, and schedule checksum. These files
can be inspected before any model calls are made.

## Run a small pilot before the complete schedule

A two-question pilot tests the procedure with 24 calls before the complete
schedule is run:

```bash
python -m position_bias_demo.build_schedule \
  --input-dir INPUT \
  --output-dir PILOT_SCHEDULE \
  --seed 20260815 \
  --question-ids 81 82 \
  --comparison-files alpaca-13b.jsonl
```

This produces 24 trials. Run them through the model route prepared earlier in
the series:

```bash
python -m position_bias_demo.run_trials \
  --schedule PILOT_SCHEDULE/trials.jsonl \
  --build-manifest PILOT_SCHEDULE/build-manifest.json \
  --run-dir PILOT_RUN \
  --model vllm-local \
  --temperature 0
```

The model name must match an entry in `model-registry.yaml`. A direct hosted
API, Ollama, or Azure can be used by substituting the corresponding registry
name. The resulting model
environment will differ from the one used for the Qwen result reported below.

Each completed trial is saved immediately. If the run is interrupted, the
collector checks the existing records and continues with the next unfinished
trial. Progress can be viewed without displaying prompts or responses:

```bash
python -m position_bias_demo.track_run \
  --run-dir PILOT_RUN \
  --verify
```

Once the pilot has been checked, the same collection command can be used with
`OUTPUT/trials.jsonl` and a new run directory to collect the full schedule.

## The parser does not infer a verdict from the explanation

The judge explains its decision and ends the response with `[[A]]` or `[[B]]`.
The complete response is saved before this verdict is extracted.

The primary rule accepts exactly one case-sensitive `[[A]]` or `[[B]]`. A
missing verdict, a tie, or more than one verdict is marked invalid. The analysis
does not infer a choice from the explanation or ask the model again.

The analysis is also repeated with FastChat's original parsing rule. This is a
**sensitivity analysis**: it checks whether using a different reasonable
scoring decision changes the result. The implementation difference is
documented in the
[`material audit`](../../llm-behavior-demos/materials/position-bias/AUDIT.md).

Run the analysis with:

```bash
python -m position_bias_demo.analyze \
  --schedule OUTPUT/trials.jsonl \
  --run-dir FULL_RUN \
  --output-dir ANALYSIS
```

## How the result terms are used

Two different things can remain the “same,” so the results use more specific
language:

- **A/B positional option** refers to the visible position. A is the first
  answer shown and B is the second.
- **Underlying answer content (X/Y)** refers to the actual archived model
  response, regardless of whether it appears in positional option A or B.
- **Same A/B positional option across repetitions** means that Qwen repeated
  its choice when the entire prompt, including answer order, was unchanged. The
  content and position are both the same in this comparison.
- **Answer-identity consistency after a swap** means that Qwen continued to
  prefer the same underlying answer content—X or Y—after it moved from A to B,
  or from B to A.
- **Positional-option consistency after a swap** means that Qwen continued to
  choose A or continued to choose B after X and Y exchanged positions. The
  displayed position stayed the same, but the underlying answer identity
  changed. Across pairs, this pattern reveals a positional preference.

The repetition result concerns the first comparison. The answer-order result
concerns the second.

## What happened in the completed Qwen run

The completed demonstration used `Qwen/Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct`, served locally
with vLLM 0.22.1. Temperature was set to 0, and the maximum response length was
1,024 tokens. All 432 scheduled judgments were collected, and every response
contained a valid verdict under the primary parser.

### Were identical trials repeatable?

Yes, in most cases. The 36 answer pairs were each shown in two orders, creating
72 distinct comparison prompts. Each exact prompt was sent to Qwen three times.
The answer order remained fixed across these repetitions. Selecting the same
option therefore meant choosing the same content in the same position; this
measure cannot separate the two. It only shows whether Qwen repeated its choice
when nothing in the prompt changed.

With the original judge instructions, Qwen selected the same A/B positional
option on all three repetitions for 66 of the 72 prompts. For the remaining
six, it selected one option twice and the other once. This produced a
repetition-stability score of 97.2%.

### Did swapping the answers change the judgment?

Swapping the answers changed Qwen's preferred answer identity for 12 of the 36
pairs. In these cases, Qwen kept choosing the same A/B positional option after
X and Y exchanged positions. Six pairs consistently favored A, the first
position, and six consistently favored B, the second position.

For the remaining 24 pairs, Qwen continued to prefer the same underlying
answer content, X or Y, after it moved to the other position. These were
answer-identity-consistent choices.

{% include figure.liquid
  path="assets/img/blog/position-bias-results.svg"
  alt="With the original prompt, Qwen retained the same underlying answer identity after the position swap for 24 of 36 pairs and retained the same displayed position for 12. After the warning was removed, the counts were 25 and 11."
  caption="Figure 3. With the original prompt, Qwen retained the same underlying answer identity after the position swap for 24 of 36 pairs and retained the same displayed position for 12. After the warning was removed, the counts were 25 and 11."
  loading="lazy"
%}

The result was therefore not driven by a single overall preference for the
first or the second position.

### Did removing the warning make a clear difference?

No clear difference appeared in this small sample. After the warning was
removed, Qwen preferred the same underlying answer content across positions for
25 of the 36 pairs, compared with 24 under the original prompt. The number of
pairs in which it switched to the other content fell from 12 to 11. Identical
repetitions also remained similar: the same A/B positional option was selected
on all three repetitions for 67 of 72 prompts, compared with 66 of 72 before
the warning was removed.

A one-pair difference is not enough to conclude that removing the warning
increased or reduced position bias. Repeating the analysis with FastChat's
original parser produced the same result.

### How this result relates to the published study

Shi et al. found that all 15 judges sometimes changed their choice after the
answers were swapped. Depending on the judge, the same underlying answer was
retained in 57% to 82% of comparisons
([Shi et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07791#S3.SS2)). Qwen retained
the same underlying answer for 24 of 36 pairs, or 66.7%, in this demonstration.
These percentages are not directly comparable because the models, questions,
answers, and generation settings differ.

are available in
